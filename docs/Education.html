Education

Chavez lab

As a research assistant in the lab of Dr. Dario Chavez, my work focused primarily on understanding the population structure of peaches and other Prunus species. I did this through the use of molecular marker data, as well as historical pedigree data, and applied my analysis to our own peach breeding program as well as worldwide peach populations.

Peaches, Prunus persica, were originally domesticated in China thousands of years ago. The process of domestication created trees with superior fruit, at the cost of genetic diversity. While peaches have since been carried across the world, China remains the cradle of P. persica diversity, and therefore a source of novel traits and possible disease resistance. Adding Chinese peaches to our germplasm collection would strengthen US peach production, but it is difficult for scientists to travel to China to collect samples. Dr. Chavez believes that the next best source may be Australia, due to a gold rush in the 1800s which brought Chinese workers, who themselves carried seeds to this new country. Using Capture-Seq technology, we have genotyped 250 Prunus accessions from across the world. The SNP markers derived from this diverse pool have been used to evaluate population structure across continents, affirming that feral peach populations in Australia may represent genetic material distinct from what is present in Europe and North America. In the future, these populations may constitute a valuable source of genetic diversity which could bolster peach breeding and aid in the development of new, stronger, and more desirable varieties of peaches.

PhD, North Carolina State University

As of August 2025, I am currently pursuing a PhD in Horticulture at North Carolina State University. As a research assitant in the lab of Dr. Hsuan Chen, I am studying native ornamental plants and working on developing molecular markers that can assist in selection and breeding, as well as doing physiological studies of several native plants.

I have also served as a committee member to help plant the 8th Biennial NCSU Plant Breeding Club Symposium.

Chen Lab

I am still in the early stages of my PhD reseach, but the projects I am planning to work on are as follows:

  1. Marker-trait association in Cercis canadensis (Eastern redbud) using SNP markers derived from a diverse Cercis panel. The goal is to identify markers associated with traits like flower color and leaf glossiness, then develop KASP primers and other tools for marker assisted selection.

  2. FISH characterization of Cercis species. Using probes targeting the 5s and 5.8s rDNA regions, we plan to fluorescently label chromosomes from six different species in the genus Cercis. This will allow us to differentiate between species and between individual chromosomes, allowing us to make more informed breeding decisions.

  3. GISH characterization of Cercis hybrids. Hybridization of the species C. canadensis and C. chinensis is of interest to our lab. C. canadensis contains many valuable ornamental traits, like variable flower color and weeping architecture, while C. chinensis is hardy, resistant to some diseases, and has a novel, shrubby structure. GISH analysis of several interspecific hybrids can allow us to track crossing-over and visualize the ratio of genetic material from each parent in F1 and BC1 offspring.

  4. Native ornamental species breeding. Several species native it North Carolina, including Amelanchia species (serviceberry), have potential as ornamental plants. However, lack of genetic material, germination protocols, and established breeding strategies make these species recalcitrant to cultivar development. Collecting material, developing protocols, and conducting gamma and oryzalin treatments will help further the development of these species as native ornamentals.

  5. Cercis species rooting capabilities. C. canadensis and C. chinensis are propagated commercially across the country. They are primarily propagated by grafting, which can be time consuming and labor intensive. C. canadensis is known to have a limited capacity for rooting from cuttings, although C. chinensis is more capable of rooting. The question is, can rooting ability be reliably conferred to interspecific hybrid offspring. By collecting phenotypic data on rooting ability, and using the SNP markers developed earlier for Cercis, we plan to conduct GWAS to identify markers associated with rooting ability.
